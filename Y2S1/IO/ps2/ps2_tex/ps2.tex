\section*{Problem 2: Estimation Techniques}
\subsection*{Part A: OLS}

To estimate via OLS, we estimate the following equation:
\begin{equation}
  Y_{it} = e^{\alpha_0 + \omega_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}} K_{it}^{\alpha_k} L_{it}^{\alpha_l}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \alpha_0 + \omega_{it} + \alpha_k k_{it} + \alpha_l l_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
Where $y$ is log of value-added output, $k$ is log capital, $l$ is log labor, and $\omega$ is the productivity shock. We estimate this equation for both Colombia and Chile,
first homogenous across industries, then heterogenous by industry.

\subsection*{Part C: OP Estimation}

Now, when we estiamte with the Olley-Pakes method, we follow the two-step procedure outlined in the literature.
In the first step, we estimate the production function parameters using a non-parametric approach to control for the endogeneity of input choices.
In the second step, we use the estimated parameters to recover the productivity shocks.

That is, we estimate the following equation:
\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \alpha_l l_{it} + \phi(i_{it}, k_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
Where $\phi = \alpha_0 + \alpha_k k_{it} + \alpha_i i_{it} + h(i_t, k_{it})$. We estimate this equation for both Colombia and Chile, first homogenous across industries, then heterogenous by industry.       
We estimate via a second stage regression to recover $\alpha_k$ and $\alpha_{\phi}$:
\begin{equation}
  y_{t+1} + \alpha_l l_{t+1} = \alpha_k k_{t+1} + \alpha_i i_{t+1} g(\phi_t - \alpha_i i_t - \alpha_k k_t - \alpha_0) + \varepsilon_{t+1}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Part D: LP Estimation}

To estimate via Levinsohn-Petrin, we modify our previous equation to include intermediate inputs as a proxy for unobserved productivity shocks:
\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \alpha_l l_{it} + \phi(m_{it}, k_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
Where $m$ is log intermediate inputs. We estimate this equation for both Colombia and Chile, first homogenous across industries, then heterogenous by industry.

We then estimate via a second stage regression to recover $\alpha_k$ and $\alpha_{\phi}$:
\begin{equation}
  y_{t+1} + \alpha_l l_{t+1} = \alpha_k k_{t+1} + \alpha_m m_{t+1} g(\phi_t - \alpha_m m_t - \alpha_k k_t - \alpha_0) + \varepsilon_{t+1}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Part E: ACF Estimation}

To estimate via Ackerberg-Caves-Frazer, we follow a similar two-step procedure as in the OP method, but we also account for the simultaneity bias in input choices.
In the first step, we now include intermediate inputs in the non-parametric function, $\phi$, for estimation:
\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_k k_{it} + \alpha_l l_{it} + \alpha_m m_{it} + f^{-1}(m_{it}, k_{it}, l_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}

Which can be used to recover productivity shocks as:
\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \phi(m_{it}, k_{it}, l_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}  
\end{equation}

The second stage is now different, using lagged inputs as instruments to control for simultaneity bias:
\begin{equation}
  y_t = \alpha_k k_t + \alpha_l l_t + g(\phi_{t-1} - \alpha_k k_{t-1} - \alpha_m m_{t-1} - \alpha_l l_{t-1} - \alpha_0) + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Discussion}

In Colombia, average productivity trends upward over the time horizon, with a slightly lesser increase in standard deviation, indicating that the firms
are becoming more productive on average with time, while variability is not increasing to the same degree. In Chile, we see a a spike in productivity after
1985, continuing until 1990 before slowing. Standard deviation also increases over time, with more consistency in growth. This suggests that Chilean
firms are also becoming more productive on average, but with greater variability among firms.

When splitting out by industry, we see that Colombian textile industries are the most productive on average, while wood and apparel are the least. However,
teh textile industry also exhibits the highest variability, while food is the least so. In Chile, all industries are increasing in productivity over time to similar
degrees, with textiles being the most productive until about 1992 when food overtakes it. Apparel is the least productive until about 1994, when metal
takes over as least prductive. Food and apparel are the most variable industries in Chile when looking at growth in standard deviation, though textiles
have the highest level until about 1990. Over the whole horizon, wood and metal are the least variable.
