---
title: "Subsidizing Health Insurance for Low-Income Adults: Evidence from Massachusetts"
author: "Tate Mason"
---

# Broad Question

Finklestein and co-authors are broadly concerned with parsing how much low-income agents will pay for health insurance, and the implications these findings may have on insurance markets.

# Specific Question

In 2006, Massachusetts implemented policy which subsidizes healthcare for low-income individuals. This policy is used as a natural experiment to use RDD to recover estimates for the consumer demand for these subsidized plans, known by CommCare, focusing on the 2011 fiscal year. 

# Policy Implications

Gaining insight into state subsidized insurance programs can serve as backing for potential legislation, thus providing some sort of basis upon which policymakers can leverage towards passing/blocking a similar program. Further, insight into consumer demand for such a program would be valuable for ascertaining efficacy of other insurance legislation.

# Economic Implications

Understanding the insurance market is important, especially in this environment with a natural experiment. Further, they find adverse selection patterns, which are important to understand when creating pricing/demand models in any market.

# What We Learn

As income grows towards the cap of 300% of the FPL, enrollment in the program drops significantly while at the same time, insurer costs rise with income (imprecise above 200%). Thus, they find an adverse selection story, where because those higher up are less willing to enroll, costs are passed to those who need lowered insurance costs. Enrollment falls by 20-30% at the first threshold (above 150%). Broadly, there is a gap in willingness to pay and the costs associated with a program like CommCare. One explanation is a underestimation of duration of unemployment, with the unemployed population underestimating their time unemployed by 70%. This is a possible explanation of the gap between WTP and costs. Further, they explore behavioral biases and the access to uncompensated care, but are unable to come to quantitative conclusions.

# Empirical Strategy

Implementation of a Regression Discontinuity Design

# Potential Pitfalls

Assumption of compliance with reporting protocol is large in this context, with those lower in the income distribution more likely to have unofficial income sources. However, they argue their data does not show this behavior.

# Overcome

They overcome by backing their data, and using other estimates to robustness test. They recover similar estimates.
